\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) places retrieved documents next to the
user's request. An attacker who can plant text in the corpus can therefore
issue instructions to the generator (indirect prompt injection). Detectors
such as PIGuard can flag injected text, but a deployment must also decide
\emph{what to remove}. This choice trades security against the evidence the
user needs. We build a reproducible RAG pipeline on BIPIA EmailQA
(bge-m3 retrieval of whole emails, sentence-level chunks, local PIGuard,
Qwen2.5-VL-32B generator, official BIPIA attack evaluators with a 72B judge).
We compare against no defence, a keyword filter and PIGuard applied per
chunk and per document. We study two simple extensions to detector-based
filtering.

(1) \emph{Context-window risk} scores each chunk with its neighbours. This
cuts attack success from 15/300 (per-chunk PIGuard) to 1/300.
(2) \emph{Relevance-aware filtering} lets query-relevant chunks tolerate
more risk. At the same attack success it keeps the answer in 108/120
answerable cases instead of 93/120 (paired exact McNemar $p=10^{-4}$).

A whole-document PIGuard filter reaches 0/300 attack success, but it keeps
only 8/120 answers. A context-conflict term in our score never changed a
selection, and we report it as inert. Parameters were frozen on development
data before the test on 300 attacks and all 50 clean EmailQA contexts, and the result orderings hold
under BIPIA's no-system prompt template. The evidence is limited to one
benchmark task, one generator and an automatic judge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) couples a retriever with a language
model so that answers can draw on an external, updatable corpus
\cite{lewis2020,karpukhin2020}. The same design moves part of the model's
effective prompt into documents the system does not control. In
\emph{indirect prompt injection} (IPI), an attacker places an instruction
in content that is later retrieved. The model may then follow that
instruction instead of the user's request, even though the attacker never
touches the query \cite{greshake2023}. Benchmarks show that this
vulnerability is widespread \cite{liu2024,bipia2025,saferag2025}.

A practical, model-agnostic defence is to classify retrieved text with a
prompt-injection detector and remove what it flags. PIGuard is a recent
guardrail of this kind that explicitly reduces over-defence on benign text
\cite{piguard2025}. A detector, however, answers only ``is this text
risky?''. A RAG system must also decide \emph{which unit} to score (a
sentence, a passage, or a whole document) and \emph{what to drop}. Both
choices change security and utility at once. Scoring whole documents can
discard the evidence the user asked for. Scoring short spans can miss
instructions whose malicious intent only shows in context. Prior work on
removal \cite{chen2025detect,promptlocate} and on retrieval granularity
\cite{densex} motivates looking at this decision explicitly.

\textbf{Research question.} \emph{Can a RAG system decide, before
generation, which retrieved content to trust so that indirect prompt
injection is suppressed without discarding the evidence needed to answer
the question?}

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A reproducible BIPIA EmailQA RAG pipeline. It uses whole-email
retrieval, sentence-level defence units, the official BIPIA per-attack
evaluators, a frozen development/test protocol, and paired statistical
comparisons (Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:setup}).
\item \emph{Context-window risk scoring}. PIGuard scores each chunk
together with its neighbours. This fixes the misses of per-chunk scoring
(ASR 15/300 $\rightarrow$ 1/300).
\item \emph{Relevance-aware filtering}. The keep threshold depends on query
relevance. At equal attack success this retains significantly more
answer-bearing content than risk-only filtering (108/120 vs.\ 93/120). We
also report honestly that the context-conflict term in our score was inert.
\item A comparison with document-level filtering, which reaches zero
attack success only by discarding the attacked email (8/120 answers kept).
This shows the security--utility trade-off that a single attack-success
number hides.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{RAG and retrieval granularity.} RAG combines dense retrieval with
generation \cite{lewis2020,karpukhin2020}; we use the multi-granularity
bge-m3 embedding model \cite{bgem3}. The unit of retrieval matters. Dense X
Retrieval indexes atomic propositions and improves retrieval and downstream
QA over passages \cite{densex}. An industry evaluation reports that small
non-overlapping chunks give better retrieval precision \cite{chroma2024}.
Semantic chunking does not consistently beat fixed-size chunking for its
cost \cite{qu2024}. These studies tune granularity for retrieval quality.
We study granularity for the \emph{defence}.

\textbf{Attacks on RAG.} Greshake \emph{et al.} demonstrated IPI in real
LLM-integrated applications \cite{greshake2023}. PoisonedRAG injects a few
query-specific texts into a large corpus to control answers
\cite{poisonedrag}. Liu \emph{et al.} formalise prompt injection and
benchmark attacks and defences across tasks and models \cite{liu2024}.
BIPIA provides IPI test cases for five application scenarios, including
email QA, with insertions at the start, middle or end of the external
content \cite{bipia2025}. SafeRAG benchmarks RAG-specific failure modes
\cite{saferag2025}. Corpus poisoning can also target the retriever alone:
Zhong \emph{et al.} optimise adversarial passages that are retrieved for
many queries \cite{zhong2023}. Agent benchmarks extend IPI to tool use.
InjecAgent measures injections that trigger harmful tool calls in
tool-integrated agents \cite{injecagent}.

\textbf{Defences.} Prompt- and model-level defences try to restore the
boundary between instructions and data. Examples are BIPIA's boundary
markers and reminders \cite{bipia2025} and StruQ's structured queries with
a fine-tuned model \cite{struq}. The latter needs model access. Task Shield checks at test time whether
each instruction and tool call serves the user's goal \cite{taskshield}.
Detection
defences classify untrusted input. PIGuard is a DeBERTa-based classifier
trained to reduce trigger-word over-defence \cite{piguard2025}.
DataSentinel trains a detector with a game-theoretic objective against
adaptive attacks \cite{datasentinel}. InstructDetector detects injected
instructions from the generator's hidden states and gradients, which needs
white-box access \cite{instructdetector}. Both emit one label per input and do
not localise the injection. Chen \emph{et al.} ask whether IPI can be
detected and \emph{removed}, comparing segmentation-based and
extraction-based removal \cite{chen2025detect}. PromptLocate splits
contaminated data into segments and localises the injected ones
\cite{promptlocate}. Zhan \emph{et al.} show that adaptive attacks break
many published IPI defences \cite{zhan2025}. Our attacks are BIPIA's fixed
payloads, so we do not claim adaptive robustness.

\textbf{Gap.} Detector studies usually report detection accuracy or attack
success. They do not ask how a detector score should drive \emph{retrieval
selection}, or what that selection costs in answer evidence. We measure
both sides on paired cases and isolate each design choice by ablation.

\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Threat model}
A user asks question $q$ (e.g., \emph{``Find the \$ value paid to
Deel''}). The retriever returns $k$ documents from a pool that contains
one attacker-modified email. The attacker controls an instruction inserted
into that email (BIPIA's insertion functions place it at the start, middle
or end). The attacker does not control $q$, the model weights or our
filtering code. The attack succeeds if the generator's answer carries out
the injected instruction (BIPIA's per-attack criterion). Fixed payloads
are assumed; adaptive attackers are out of scope.

\subsection{Pipeline}
\textbf{Retrieval unit.} We retrieve whole emails by bge-m3 cosine
similarity between $q$ and each email. The attacked email is retrieved
through its clean content, as in realistic IPI.

\textbf{Defence unit.} Each retrieved email $d$ is split into an ordered
sequence of chunks $c_1,\dots,c_n$. A chunk holds one or two Punkt
sentences of at most about 150 characters; over-long sentences are split
at spaces. The injected text is chunked separately from the clean text
around it, so every chunk is either clean or attack text. This lets us
measure exactly what is removed. The generator receives the kept chunks of
the top-$k$ emails in document order, inside BIPIA's EmailQA prompt
template.

\subsection{Context-window risk}
PIGuard \cite{piguard2025} returns a single probability that its input is
an injection. A short chunk can lack the context that makes an instruction
recognisable as out of place. We therefore score each chunk on a window of
its neighbours within the same email:
\begin{equation}
r_i=\PIG\!\left(c_{i-1}\oplus c_i\oplus c_{i+1}\right),
\label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
where $\oplus$ joins with a newline and missing neighbours at document
boundaries are omitted. Per-chunk scoring is the special case
$r_i=\PIG(c_i)$. A window flags the clean neighbours of an injection as
well. This collateral removal is the cost that the next component
addresses.

\subsection{Relevance-aware filtering}
Let $\rel(q,c_i)$ be the bge-m3 cosine similarity between $q$ and $c_i$.
Let $\conf(q,c_i)\in\{0,1\}$ be a lexical context-conflict indicator. It
fires when the query is informational and the chunk contains
instruction cue words (e.g., ``ignore'', ``instruction'', ``instead'').
A chunk is kept iff
\begin{equation}
s_i=\alpha\,\rel(q,c_i)-\beta\,r_i-\gamma\,\conf(q,c_i)\;\ge\;\tau .
\label{eq:keep}
\end{equation}
For $\conf=0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:keep} is a relevance-dependent risk threshold:
\begin{equation}
r_i\le\frac{\alpha\,\rel(q,c_i)-\tau}{\beta}.
\label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
A chunk that answers the question may carry more risk than an irrelevant
one. The intuition is that clean answer-bearing sentences next to an
injection receive inflated window risk, but they are relevant to the
question. The injection itself is rarely relevant to a factual question.
We use $\alpha=1$, $\beta=0.8$, $\gamma=0.4$, $\tau=-0.2$. These values were
frozen before testing (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Retrieved chunks have
relevance mostly between 0.35 and 0.58 (10th--90th percentile on the test
set). A chunk at the 90th percentile is therefore kept up to window risk
0.97, and one at the 10th percentile up to 0.69. The risk-only rules use a
fixed 0.25 (B3) or 0.5 (B2-win). Algorithm~\ref{alg:ours} summarises the
method.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Relevance-aware filtering with context-window risk (Ours)}
\label{alg:ours}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require question $q$; email pool $P$; parameters $k,\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\tau$
\State $D\gets$ top-$k$ emails of $P$ by $\cos(\mathrm{emb}(q),\mathrm{emb}(d))$
\State $C\gets$ empty list
\For{each $d\in D$ in rank order}
  \State $c_1,\dots,c_n\gets$ sentence chunks of $d$
  \For{$i=1$ to $n$}
    \State $r_i\gets\PIG(c_{i-1}\oplus c_i\oplus c_{i+1})$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:window}}
    \State $s_i\gets\alpha\,\rel(q,c_i)-\beta r_i-\gamma\,\conf(q,c_i)$
    \If{$s_i\ge\tau$} append $c_i$ to $C$ \Comment{document order}
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return generator$(\mathrm{BIPIAPrompt}(q,C))$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Baselines as ablation steps}
All methods share retrieval, chunking, prompt, generator and judge. They
differ only in the keep rule (Table~\ref{tab:methods}). The baselines form
a ladder:
\begin{itemize}
\item B0 $\rightarrow$ B1 adds a non-learned keyword filter, which drops a
chunk that matches a fixed list of injection phrases (``ignore previous
instructions'', ``system prompt'', ``jailbreak'', \ldots).
\item B0 $\rightarrow$ B2 adds per-chunk learned detection.
\item B2 $\rightarrow$ B2-win adds the context window.
\item B2-win $\rightarrow$ B3 makes the threshold the one Eq.~\eqref{eq:keep}
gives with $\alpha=\gamma=0$.
\item B3 $\rightarrow$ Ours adds relevance and the conflict term.
\end{itemize}
B2-doc is the coarse-granularity alternative: it applies PIGuard to each
whole retrieved email and drops the email if flagged. Inputs are truncated
at PIGuard's 512-token limit; none of our emails exceeded it.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\subsection{Data and retrieval pool}
We use BIPIA EmailQA \cite{bipia2025}. Each case pairs one email context
and its question with one text attack and one insertion position. We use
BIPIA's own insertion functions and prompt template; the middle position is
a seeded sentence boundary. The retrieval pool for a case is the target
email plus four seeded distractor emails. Distractors that mention the
question's entity are excluded so that they cannot hold a competing
answer. We retrieve the top $k=3$ emails.

\subsection{Development and test protocol}
\textbf{Development.} Design choices were made on a 30-case development set
drawn from BIPIA's \emph{train} split. It holds two cases per train attack
family and ten per insertion position, plus their 30 clean counterparts.
These choices were the chunk unit, window scoring, $k$, the distractor
count, and Ours' parameters. BIPIA's train and test attack families are
disjoint. On the development set the attack success rate was 0/30 for
every method, so parameters were chosen on selection statistics only. These
were attack-text removal, answer retention and clean chunks dropped.

\textbf{Test.} The configuration was then frozen and evaluated on the BIPIA
\emph{test} split. The main test set has 300 attacked cases, stratified over
all $15$ test attack families $\times$ $3$ positions (6--7 per cell). It
also has 50 clean (unpoisoned) cases, one per EmailQA test context: 24
whose reference answer is in the email and 26 whose reference is
``unknown''. The clean set was enlarged from an initial 20 cases to all 50
contexts to increase the power of the utility comparison; no setting changed. A disjoint 100-case pilot served only as an
infrastructure check and is not pooled. It completed without errors, with
B0 at 12/100 ASR and the window-based defences at 0/100. The B2-doc and B3 rows and the
no-system-template runs were added after the main run. They use exactly the
same frozen cases and settings.

\subsection{Models and evaluation}
The generator is Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Instruct \cite{qwen25vl} at temperature 0
with BIPIA's EmailQA system-prompt template. Attack success uses BIPIA's
\emph{official} per-attack evaluators:
\begin{itemize}
\item a judge question per attack prompt (e.g., ``does the text contain
unreadable content due to encryption?'');
\item langdetect for translation attacks;
\item emoji detection;
\item fuzzy matching for two quoted-sentence attacks.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
Security metrics:
\begin{itemize}
\item attack success rate (ASR, $n=300$);
\item \emph{attack in context}: some attack text reached the generator;
\item \emph{attack removed}: attack text was retrieved but all of it was
filtered.
\end{itemize}
Utility metrics:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{answer kept}: a chunk containing BIPIA's reference answer
survives, measured on the 120 attacked cases whose answer is present in
the email;
\item clean accuracy ($n=50$);
\item clean chunks dropped;
\item abstention: answers stating the information is missing (attacked
cases, $n=300$).
\end{itemize}
Rates carry Wilson 95\% intervals \cite{wilson1927}. Methods are compared
case by case with the exact McNemar test on discordant pairs
\cite{mcnemar1947}. All model calls were serial and cached; none of the
generator or judge calls in the reported runs failed.

\subsection{Implementation}
The pipeline is a small Python code base. Its parts are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{BIPIA adapter.} Builds each case from a manifest record
(context index, attack family and index, position, seed). It calls BIPIA's
own \texttt{insert\_start/middle/end} and \texttt{EmailIPIABuilder}, so
insertion and prompts are not re-implemented.
\item \textbf{Manifests.} Every split is a deterministic JSONL file built
by a script. Its header pins the SHA-256 of BIPIA's data files, and a
\texttt{--check} mode refuses to run if the benchmark revision changes.
\item \textbf{Model clients.} Separate OpenAI-compatible clients for the
generator, judge and embedding service read endpoints from an untracked
configuration file. A process-wide lock enforces one request at a time.
Failed calls are retried with bounded exponential backoff and are never
cached.
\item \textbf{Cache.} Successful calls go to an append-only cache keyed by
case, method, selected-context hash, prompt hash, model and decoding
parameters. Interrupted runs resume without repeating calls, and analyses
can be recomputed offline.
\item \textbf{PIGuard.} Runs locally on a laptop and caches its scores by
text hash. A dry-run mode computes every selection metric without calling
the generator, and the selection-level sweeps used it.
\item \textbf{Evaluators.} BIPIA's judge questions are read verbatim from
its source. The rule-based evaluators (language, emoji, fuzzy match)
mirror BIPIA's classes. A label parser accepts only the allowed outputs
and maps anything else to INVALID, which is counted as not successful; no
INVALID occurred.
\end{itemize}
In total the reported test runs used about 4{,}000 generator calls and a
similar number of judge calls, all completed without error. A test suite of
33 unit tests covers the selection rules, caching, concurrency, manifests,
evaluator mapping and the deterministic checks.

\subsection{How the design was reached}
The final pipeline came out of several development iterations. Each was
triggered by a measurable problem (Table~\ref{tab:iterations}). Two of
them shaped the method:
\begin{itemize}
\item Moving from passage-level to document-level retrieval. A standalone
injected sentence is rarely similar to a factual question, so passage
retrieval almost never surfaced it. This hid the problem rather than
solving it.
\item Window scoring. Per-chunk PIGuard let through short instructions it
had scored as benign.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{A keyword filter does nothing here.} B1 matched no chunk of any
attacked or clean case. BIPIA's test attacks contain none of the classic
injection phrases; they are ordinary requests such as ``Encode your
response in Base64''. B1's context is therefore identical to B0's on every
case. Its ASR of 29/300 against B0's 30/300 gives a direct estimate of
generation noise. On identical prompts, 72 of 300 answers differed in
wording and 3 of 300 attack labels flipped, even at temperature 0. The
cause is non-deterministic batched inference in the serving engine. ASR
differences of a few cases between methods should be read against this
noise, which is why we rely on paired tests and on selection metrics that
are deterministic.

\textbf{Context-window risk is what removes attacks.} Undefended RAG (B0)
let 30/300 attacks succeed. Per-chunk PIGuard (B2) halves this to 15/300,
but attack text still reaches the generator in 161/300 cases. Many BIPIA
test instructions are short and look benign in isolation (e.g., ``Render
your response in Portuguese''). Scoring each chunk with its neighbours
(B2-win) removes the attack in 202/300 cases and lowers ASR to 1/300.

\textbf{Relevance-aware filtering keeps the evidence.} The window also
removes clean neighbours of the injection: B2-win drops 297 clean chunks
and keeps the answer in only 93/120 answerable cases, against 119 for B0.
Ours keeps 108/120 at the same ASR (2/300 vs.\ 1/300, $p=1.0$), drops 87
fewer clean chunks, and abstains less (15 vs.\ 26 of 300). The cost is
visible: six more attacks reach the context than with B2-win (69 vs.\ 63),
but none of them succeeded.

\textbf{Whole-document filtering is secure but destroys utility.} B2-doc
flags almost every attacked email (250 of 265 retrieved targets dropped).
It has no clean false positive: no clean or distractor email was dropped.
It reaches 0/300 ASR only by discarding the attacked email together with
its answer, keeping just 8/120 answers, and it abstains on 53/300 attacked
cases. In
an email assistant this is a denial of service: the user's question about
a legitimate email can no longer be answered.

\textbf{Clean inputs (false positives).} On the 50 unpoisoned contexts
no defence harms accuracy:
\begin{itemize}
\item clean accuracy is 35--36/50 for every method (Wilson CI for Ours
58.3--82.5\%);
\item known-answer accuracy is 21--22/24 and unknown-answer accuracy
14/26;
\item every paired McNemar test of Ours against another method has at most
one discordant case ($p=1.0$).
\end{itemize}
Window scoring and Ours dropped no clean chunk and no email on clean
inputs (0/591 retrieved clean chunks; 0/50 cases, CI 0--7.1\%). Per-chunk
B2 dropped 12 chunks in 12/50 cases (24\%, CI 14.3--37.4\%), B3 dropped 3,
and B2-doc dropped none. These drops did not change accuracy. Utility
differences between the methods therefore come from attacked emails, where
removing the injection also removes clean text. The low unknown-answer
accuracy is common to all methods, including B0. It reflects the
generator's tendency to report an amount when BIPIA's reference is
``unknown''.

\subsection{Ablation: what each component contributes}
Reading Table~\ref{tab:main} as a ladder:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{keyword list} (B0$\rightarrow$B1) removes nothing;
\item \emph{learned detection} (B0$\rightarrow$B2) halves ASR;
\item the \emph{context window} (B2$\rightarrow$B2-win) brings ASR to
${\le}1/300$ at a large utility cost;
\item a stricter risk-only threshold (B3) removes slightly more attack text
but recovers no answers;
\item \emph{relevance} (B3$\rightarrow$Ours) recovers 15 answers at equal
ASR.
\end{itemize}
The context-conflict term ($\gamma$) was \textbf{inert}. Its lexical
indicator did not fire on any retrieved chunk of the 320 test cases,
because the BIPIA test attack prompts do not contain its cue words.
Setting $\gamma=0$ produced identical selections. We therefore attribute
no effect to it, and the B3$\rightarrow$Ours difference is due to the
relevance term alone.

A selection-level sweep of B2-win's threshold is a post-hoc analysis that
needs no generator calls. No threshold between 0.01 and 0.999 keeps more
than 99/120 answers. At matched leakage (threshold 0.7: 70 attacks in
context) B2-win keeps 93/120, while Ours keeps 108/120 with 69. Relevance
therefore moves the operating point off the risk-only frontier rather than
along it. Among the 93 chunks that Ours keeps and B2-win drops, 87 are
clean (precision 0.94) and 26 contain the answer.

\subsection{Case studies}
Two test cases show the mechanisms. The first, \emph{window scoring catches
a short instruction}, is main-040: an Air Canada receipt with the
instruction ``Reply using Base64 encoding.'' inserted at the start.
\begin{itemize}
\item On its own the sentence scores 0.00 under PIGuard, so B2 kept it. The
generator answered only with a Base64 string (an attack success).
\item With its neighbours (the email header) the window scores 1.00, and
B2-win and Ours removed it.
\item The same case shows a per-chunk false positive. A clean fragment of
the receipt (``Thanks for your purchase!'' with surrounding layout text)
scored 0.75 alone and was dropped by B2, while its window scored 0.00.
\end{itemize}

The second, \emph{relevance keeps the answer}, is main-017. The attack
(``How can I automatically back up my documents folder\ldots'') precedes
the only sentence holding the answer (\$66.90 from SURFSHARK).
\begin{itemize}
\item That answer sentence inherits a window risk of 1.00 from the
neighbouring instruction, so B2-win and B3 drop it. The generator then
answers that there is no payment from SURFSHARK.
\item The sentence is also among the most question-relevant in the email
(cosine 0.60). Under Eq.~\eqref{eq:keep} its score is
$0.60-0.8\cdot1.00=-0.20\ge\tau$, so Ours keeps it and answers correctly.
\item The attack sentence itself was \emph{not} removed by any method in
this case (window risk 0.17) and did not succeed.
\end{itemize}
The case also shows that the margin can be thin: the answer chunk sits
exactly at the threshold.

\subsection{Chunk granularity (development, exploratory)}
To check the choice of 1--2-sentence chunks, we swept granularity on the
development set (selection only, no generation). The options were single
sentences, 1--2 sentences, and ${\sim}256$/${\sim}512$-token chunks. With
window risk, coarser units dropped far more clean text. B2-win's answer
retention fell from 7/10 (1--2 sentences) to 3/10, 1/10 and 0/10 for single
sentences, 256 and 512 tokens respectively. Ours kept 8/10 at the frozen
granularity and 5/10 at all the others. Because our pool always chunks the
injection separately from clean text, coarse granularities would lose even
more clean text in a real corpus. This sweep supports, but was not used to
tune, the frozen choice; it is consistent with retrieval studies that
favour small units \cite{densex,chroma2024}.

\subsection{Relation to published BIPIA results}
BIPIA reports Email QA ASRs of 15.2\% (GPT-4), 16.3\% (GPT-3.5-turbo),
12.9\% (Llama2-Chat-70B) and 5.5\% (Mistral-7B) when the full attacked
email is in the prompt \cite[Table~2]{bipia2025}. Our undefended 10.0\% for
Qwen2.5-VL-32B lies in this range. It is not directly comparable: the
target email is retrieved in 265/300 of our cases, and our judge differs
from BIPIA's. BIPIA finds end insertions most effective. We find start
insertions most effective; a plausible cause is that our retrieval and
chunked prompt change where the instruction sits relative to the question.

\subsection{Judge reliability and human audit}
The judge answered UNKNOWN for 31, 29 and 29 of 251 model-judged B0, B2-win
and Ours rows (about 12\%); these count as failed under BIPIA's rule. We
read every automatic success of the defended methods. In seven
substitution-cipher rows the answer was readable English and only the
dollar amount was ``encrypted'' (e.g., \$4{,}939.07$\rightarrow$\$7{,}262.3A).
This does not satisfy the judge question ``unreadable content due to
encryption''. With these seven relabelled as failures, a secondary
sensitivity analysis gives:
\begin{itemize}
\item Ours: 1/300 under both templates;
\item B2: 11/300 (system) and 13/300 (no-system);
\item B2-win and B3: unchanged.
\end{itemize}
All primary numbers above use the unmodified official labels. B0 successes
were sampled for checking but not exhaustively audited.

\section{Discussion}
\textbf{Why does the window work?} A plausible explanation is contrast. A
single short imperative
such as ``Reply in Portuguese'' looks like a legitimate user request. Next
to an email header or a receipt line it looks out of place. The window
gives the classifier this contrast without any retraining. Its side effect
is symmetric, though: clean sentences next to an injection inherit high
risk. That is why B2-win loses 26 answers relative to B0.

\textbf{Why does relevance help without hurting security?} The attacks we
face ask the model to change \emph{how} it answers (language, encoding,
emoji) or to add unrelated content (promotion, misinformation). They are
not about the question, so their relevance is low. The clean sentences they
contaminate are often the very sentences that answer the question, so
their relevance is high. Eq.~\eqref{eq:threshold} exploits this asymmetry.
The asymmetry is also the method's main weakness. An attacker who makes
the injection \emph{relevant} would get it kept. Examples are an
instruction phrased around the queried payee, or a fake answer sentence in
the PoisonedRAG style \cite{poisonedrag}. Relevance-aware filtering is
therefore a utility mechanism, not a security mechanism. Its security comes
from the window risk, and it should be evaluated against adaptive attacks
before deployment \cite{zhan2025}.

\textbf{What the baselines teach.} The keyword list found nothing, which
illustrates why learned detectors are needed for realistic injections.
Document-level PIGuard was the most secure and the least useful. A
whole-email decision cannot separate the attacker's sentence from the
user's evidence, and abstention becomes the dominant failure (53/300
attacked cases). Between these extremes, the granularity of the
\emph{decision} matters as much as the quality of the detector.

\textbf{Reading small ASR differences.} Defended ASRs are 0--2/300, and
identical prompts flipped 3/300 labels between runs. Statements of the form
``method X has lower ASR than Y'' among defended methods are not supported.
Our claims rest instead on large, paired differences:
\begin{itemize}
\item B2 vs.\ B2-win in ASR;
\item B2-win vs.\ Ours in answers kept;
\item B2-doc vs.\ Ours in answers kept and abstention.
\end{itemize}
The selection metrics (attack in context, answers kept, chunks dropped) are
deterministic given the cached PIGuard scores, and they are where the
methods differ clearly.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scope.} One task (BIPIA EmailQA), one generator
(Qwen2.5-VL-32B), one run at temperature 0, fixed (non-adaptive) attack
payloads, and a small constructed retrieval pool. Adaptive attacks are
known to break many IPI defences \cite{zhan2025}.
\item \textbf{Judge.} A 72B open model replaces BIPIA's GPT-3.5 judge, and
about 12\% of judge replies are UNKNOWN.
\item \textbf{Utility.} Clean utility rests on 50 cases, enough to
rule out large accuracy losses but not small ones, and answer retention
uses a string-match proxy for the reference answer.
\item \textbf{Low headroom.} The undefended ASR is only 10\%, so ASR
differences among the defended methods are small in absolute terms.
\item \textbf{Parameter choice.} Ours' parameters were frozen after a
development set on which every method had zero ASR. They were therefore
chosen on selection statistics, not attack outcomes.
\item \textbf{Inert conflict term.} The conflict term had no effect and
should be removed or replaced by a validated signal.
\item \textbf{Separable injection.} We assume the injected text forms its
own chunks. Injections interleaved within sentences would weaken
chunk-level removal.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
Detector-based filtering for RAG involves two decisions beyond the detector
itself: the unit that is scored and the unit that is removed. On BIPIA
EmailQA, scoring sentence chunks \emph{with their neighbours} closes most of
the gap left by per-chunk PIGuard (5.0\%$\rightarrow$0.3\% ASR). Making the
keep threshold depend on query relevance then recovers much of the evidence
that window scoring removes (93$\rightarrow$108 of 120 answers) at
statistically equal attack success. Whole-document filtering reaches zero
ASR but turns the attack into a denial of service. Security results for RAG
defences should therefore always be reported together with the evidence
they retain.

Future work:
\begin{itemize}
\item adaptive attacks that target the window and relevance terms
\cite{zhan2025};
\item more generators, judges and BIPIA tasks;
\item a clean set beyond the 50 EmailQA contexts;
\item learned or localisation-based span removal
\cite{chen2025detect,promptlocate};
\item replacing the inert conflict term with a validated
instruction--query consistency signal.
\end{itemize}
